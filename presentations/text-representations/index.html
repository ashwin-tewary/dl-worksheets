From words
to meaning.

Before a model can remember a sentence, it needs something worth remembering. Build the numbers. Watch what they keep. Notice what they lose.

00 / SET THE INTENTION

Two sentences.
One missing distinction.

A search system must find related ideas. A safety system must know who bit whom. Can the same numerical representation solve both problems?

dog bites persondog: 1 · bites: 1 · person: 1
person bites dogdog: 1 · bites: 1 · person: 1

The words match. The event does not. Now consider cat and kitten: the words differ, but their meanings overlap. Today we need representations that help models use useful similarities—and recognize the information each representation discards.

Keep these two puzzles open. Can we connect different words without collapsing different stories? At the end, decide which part embeddings solve and which part needs an ordered sequence model.

By the end, you will tokenize an input, compute BoW and TF–IDF, trace a lookup and a CBOW update, explain a GloVe objective term, and choose an embedding application with a useful evaluation.

Starting vocabulary · what you need before we begin

A corpus is a collection of documents. A token is one chosen text unit; a vocabulary maps possible units to IDs. A vector is an ordered list of numbers; a matrix is a rectangular table. A sequence has positions: its order can affect the target. A timestep processes one unit; it need not be one second of clock time.

Bring basic addition, multiplication and the idea of a training objective. We introduce logarithms as a way to compress large frequency ranges, softmax as a way to turn scores into probabilities, and gradients as directions for parameter updates. Every deep derivation is optional and foldable.

This is a standalone foundation lesson. No prior RNN lecture is required. The collection's sequence-modelling and gated-RNN sheets are linked at the end as follow-on material.

01
Tokenization

Where does a word begin?

01 / THE HOOK

Is “unhappiness!” one step, two steps, or twelve? Before learning anything, a model needs us to choose its units.

02 / SEE THE PROBLEM
03 / THE IDEA

Tokenization turns text into an ordered list of units. Word tokens are intuitive but need a large vocabulary. Characters cover unfamiliar words but make long sequences. Subwords trade off both: frequent pieces can be reused in new words. A vocabulary then maps each token to an integer ID.

Think of packing a sentence into reusable tiles. Large tiles are quick to read but hard to stock; tiny tiles can spell more things but require more steps. An ID is a shelf address: token 100 is not twice as meaningful as token 50.
04 / THE FORMULA
Tokenize(x) = (t₁, …, tL)
idₖ = vocabulary[tₖ]

L is sequence length; tₖ is the kth token. The vocabulary maps token strings to integer addresses. Our word mode lowercases text and separates punctuation; it is a teaching tokenizer, not a production language tokenizer.

Go deeper · theory, derivation and worked examples

How subwords can be learned

Our miniature byte-pair encoding (BPE) example starts from character sequences for low, low, lower, each ending in ▁. Count adjacent token pairs across these three training examples; merge the most frequent pair; recount and repeat. Ties use lexicographic order of the serialized pair. The marker identifies a word boundary in this teaching convention; actual tokenizers differ.

First merge l + o (3 occurrences), then lo + w (3), then low + ▁ (2). This yields low▁ for the familiar word and reusable low inside lower. Learning the merge table and applying it to new text are separate operations. Real byte-level BPE begins from bytes and includes normalization/pretokenization choices; this simplified character example demonstrates the merge mechanism.

Sequence modelling details worth keeping

A token is not necessarily a linguistic word. Whitespace rules alone do not cover every language. Lowercasing may erase useful clues; deleting punctuation can change meaning. A fixed vocabulary needs a policy for unseen units. Some systems use an unknown token; byte coverage reduces that problem but does not make semantic understanding automatic.

Padding aligns lengths in a batch; an attention or validity mask says which positions contain real input. PAD and UNK solve different problems. Learn vocabulary/merges on the training split, then keep the same mapping at evaluation and deployment. Never infer semantic distance from numeric IDs.

02
Bag of Words

Same ingredients. Different story.

01 / THE HOOK

“dog bites person” and “person bites dog” use exactly the same words. Should a representation call them identical?

02 / SEE THE PROBLEM
03 / THE IDEA

Bag of Words represents a document with one count per vocabulary term. Every coordinate answers “how many times did this term occur?” It is a useful baseline for document classification and lexical retrieval, but its name gives away the cost: the bag forgets positions.

A shopping list counts ingredients without keeping the recipe. The same ingredients can make different dishes. Counts capture evidence about topic, not who did what to whom.
04 / THE FORMULA
xⱼ = Σk=1…L 𝟙[tₖ = vⱼ]
x ∈ ℝV

vⱼ is vocabulary term j; V is vocabulary size; 𝟙 is 1 when its condition is true and 0 otherwise. Vocabulary order must be fixed for every document.

Go deeper · theory, derivation and worked examples

A numerical example

With vocabulary [dog, bites, person], both reversed sentences produce [1, 1, 1]. “dog dog bites” becomes [2, 1, 0]. A binary presence representation clips positive counts to 1; it therefore also loses repetition.

Sparsity and local order

A vocabulary can have tens of thousands of terms while one document uses few of them. Most entries are zero: a sparse vector. Sparse storage avoids allocating every zero. Word bigrams add coordinates for adjacent pairs such as “dog bites” and “bites dog”. They restore some local order, but grow the feature space and still do not encode arbitrary long-range relationships.

Do not remove “not” just because it is common: negation may determine the label. Fit the vocabulary on training data; unknown terms at evaluation follow the declared policy. The lab deliberately ignores terms absent from its fixed vocabulary. An empty or fully unknown document becomes a zero vector.

03
TF–IDF

The loudest word is not the best clue.

01 / THE HOOK

Every document says “the”. One says “milk”. Why should a search system give those clues equal weight?

02 / SEE THE PROBLEM
03 / THE IDEA

Term frequency–inverse document frequency weights a word by its count in one document and its rarity across the corpus. A repeated word can matter inside a document; a word appearing everywhere does less to distinguish documents. This is a weighting recipe, not a learned semantic model.

Imagine a detective collecting clues. “Someone was wearing shoes” fits almost everybody; “someone carried a telescope” narrows the search. A rare clue is selective, though it may still be a typo or irrelevant.
04 / THE FORMULA
TF(t,d) = count(t,d)
IDF(t) = ln((N + 1)/(df(t) + 1)) + 1
TF–IDF(t,d) = TF(t,d) × IDF(t)

N is the number of training documents; df(t) counts documents containing t at least once. We use natural log, add-one smoothing, raw-count TF and no automatic normalization. Other conventions give different numbers.

Go deeper · theory, derivation and worked examples

Work the actual corpus

Our corpus is D1 “the cat likes milk”, D2 “the dog likes walks”, D3 “the cat likes walks”. The terms “the” and “likes” have df = 3 and IDF = 1. “cat” and “walks” have df = 2 and IDF = ln(4/3)+1 ≈ 1.288. “dog” and “milk” have df = 1 and IDF = ln(2)+1 ≈ 1.693.

Adding another “milk” to D1 doubles its raw TF weight without changing milk's document frequency. Adding “milk” to D2 changes df from 1 to 2 and reduces its IDF for every document. These are different interventions.

Conventions, normalization and leakage

Some definitions use ln(N/df), sublinear TF or L2 normalization. State the formula before comparing numbers. L2 normalization divides a nonzero vector by its norm; leave a zero vector unchanged. Cosine similarity is undefined for a zero-norm vector, so a retrieval system needs a policy.

Fit vocabulary and IDF using the training corpus only, then transform validation/test data with those frozen statistics. Computing IDF over future/test documents leaks information about their distribution. TF–IDF cannot make “kitten” match “cat” without shared features, and still ignores unigram order.

04
Word embeddings

Can different words become neighbours?

01 / THE HOOK

A one-hot vector makes cat and kitten as different as cat and telescope. Could useful coordinates put related words near one another?

02 / SEE THE PROBLEM
03 / THE IDEA

An embedding assigns each vocabulary item a dense vector. During training, a task adjusts those numbers so useful regularities can emerge. Looking up an embedding selects one row of a learned table. It does not run a dictionary definition or treat the ID as a measurement.

Think of a map whose locations are learned from how places are used. Nearby locations can share useful relationships, but north and east need not have a human-readable meaning. Rotating a map can preserve its geometry.
04 / THE FORMULA
E ∈ ℝV×d, eₖ = E[idₖ]
cos(a,b) = (a·b)/(‖a‖₂ ‖b‖₂)

V is vocabulary size; d is embedding width. L token lookups form an L × d sequence matrix. Cosine compares direction and requires two nonzero vectors. All two-dimensional vectors in this lesson are hand-designed illustrations, not trained results.

Go deeper · theory, derivation and worked examples

One-hot lookup is the same selection

For a row one-hot vector o ∈ ℝ^(1×V), oE selects one row of E and has shape 1×d. Efficient implementations gather that row directly. A V = 10,000, d = 128 table has 1,280,000 learned entries, separate from downstream model parameters.

Similarity is useful, not infallible

Cosine ignores magnitude. Related words and antonyms can appear in similar contexts; proximity does not guarantee synonymy. Axes in a learned space generally cannot be named “animalness” and “foodness”. Our 2D map exists only to make vector operations visible.

A static embedding gives bank one vocabulary row for both riverbank and financial bank uses. Contextual models produce representations that depend on the surrounding tokens. Neither a good map nor a high cosine score guarantees factual correctness, fairness or suitability for a new domain.

Retaining the L×d sequence preserves the input order for a downstream sequence model. Averaging rows produces one d-vector and loses that order. Embedding lookup alone is not sequence modelling.

05
CBOW

Hide a word. Learn from the gap.

01 / THE HOOK

If the visible neighbours are “the” and “milk”, what missing word might connect them? Where could useful word coordinates come from?

02 / SEE THE PROBLEM
03 / THE IDEA

Continuous Bag of Words trains embeddings by predicting a centre word from nearby context tokens. Gather their input embeddings, average them, compute one score per candidate target, and train against the observed target. Mistakes send gradients back into the tables.

It is a fill-in-the-blank exercise where both the answer key reader and the clue cards improve. The analogy stops at meaning: the objective rewards prediction, not an explicit understanding of grammar or truth.
04 / THE FORMULA
h = (1/C) Σc∈contextE[c]
z = Uh + b; p = softmax(z)
ℒ = −ln p[target]; θ ← θ − η∇θℒ

C is context token count; E has V×d entries; U has V×d entries for this column-vector formula; b has V entries; η is learning rate. Our exact demo uses V=3, d=2, η=0.2 and full softmax. Input and output tables are separate.

Go deeper · theory, derivation and worked examples

A complete small forward pass

Vocabulary order is [the, milk, cat]. The input rows are [1,0], [0,1], [0.2,0.4]. For context [the,milk], h=[0.5,0.5]. Output rows are [0.2,−0.1], [−0.3,0.4], [0.1,0.2], with zero biases. Scores are [0.05,0.05,0.15]. Softmax gives approximately [0.322,0.322,0.356]; the target cat loss is about 1.033.

Where the update comes from

Let g = p − onehot(target). Then ∂ℒ/∂U = ghᵀ, ∂ℒ/∂b = g, and ∂ℒ/∂h = Uᵀg. Each context occurrence receives (Uᵀg)/C in its input row. Compute all gradients from the pre-update parameters, then update simultaneously. Repeated context IDs contribute more than once.

The experiment shows actual gradient steps on one deliberately tiny training pair. Lower loss here does not demonstrate generalization, high-quality semantics or a trained language model. Reset restores all original values.

Context windows and scalable training

A symmetric window takes neighbours to both sides, so this training objective can use future context. That does not license future inputs for causal next-token inference. CBOW aggregates context without order; skip-gram instead predicts context from a centre word. Word2vec commonly uses negative sampling or hierarchical softmax to avoid full-vocabulary normalization. Negative sampling is a different objective, not the full softmax shown here.

06
GloVe

What if the whole corpus kept a ledger?

01 / THE HOOK

CBOW learns from individual prediction examples. Could the totals of which words occur near each other also teach useful vectors?

02 / SEE THE PROBLEM
03 / THE IDEA

GloVe learns word and context vectors from corpus-wide co-occurrence statistics. Each nonzero count asks a vector dot product plus biases to approximate its logarithm. Weighting prevents the objective from being dominated by an unsuitable balance of very rare or very frequent pairs.

Imagine a city ledger counting how often people visit the same neighbourhoods. We seek compact coordinates that explain the ledger. “Global” means aggregated across the corpus; the counts themselves still come from local windows.
04 / THE FORMULA
J = ΣXᵢⱼ>0 f(Xᵢⱼ)(wᵢ·w̃ⱼ + bᵢ + b̃ⱼ − ln Xᵢⱼ)²
f(x) = min(1, (x/xmax)α)

Xᵢⱼ counts context j around target i; w and w̃ are separate d-vectors; b and b̃ are scalar biases. Demo: radius-one symmetric window, unit weights, no cross-document pairs; xmax=4 and α=0.75 are teaching settings, not claimed universal defaults.

Go deeper · theory, derivation and worked examples

What exactly gets counted?

For each target position, count its immediate left and right neighbours when present. In “cat dog cat”, cat→dog occurs twice and dog→cat occurs twice. A corpus matrix can therefore be symmetric even though target and context parameter tables are separate. Repetitions affect counts. Never cross from the last token of one document into the first of the next.

Compute one objective term

In the displayed corpus, the→cat has X=2. With w=[1,0], w̃=[0,1], b=b̃=0.25, the predicted log-count is 0.5. The residual is 0.5−ln(2) ≈ −0.193. Weight f(2)=(2/4)^0.75 ≈ 0.595, so this pair contributes approximately 0.0222 to J. The lab recomputes these values for your selected pair and bias.

Zero counts are omitted: log(0) is undefined. Biases account for broad frequency effects; a dot product need not itself equal log count. Original GloVe uses a weighted least-squares objective motivated by co-occurrence ratios. We show the objective, not a proof of every semantic relation.

Compare objectives, not slogans

CBOW here minimizes negative log probability of a target given context. GloVe minimizes weighted squared errors against log co-occurrence counts. Both learn static word representations. Implementations may use distance-weighted windows and often combine learned word/context vectors at the end; our count convention and chosen pair illustration are explicit simplifications. Analogies sometimes emerge but are neither exact identities nor universal guarantees.

07
Embedding applications

Find a neighbour. Then ask what it missed.

01 / THE HOOK

Could “kitten” retrieve a document about cats without sharing an exact token? And could a high similarity score still miss the meaning of “not”?

02 / SEE THE PROBLEM
03 / THE IDEA

Embeddings support nearest-neighbour lookup, semantic retrieval, classification features and recommendation. A simple document baseline averages token vectors, then compares the result with a query using cosine similarity. It can match related coordinates beyond exact lexical overlap, but averaging cannot preserve order or every logical distinction.

A map helps you find nearby destinations; it does not tell you whether the door is open or the place is suitable. Retrieval supplies candidates. Task-specific checks and held-out evaluation decide whether the system is useful.
04 / THE FORMULA
d̄ = (1/L) Σk=1…L eₖ
score(q,d) = cos(q̄,d̄)

L counts valid in-vocabulary tokens; do not average padding. Unknown-token and empty-document policies must be explicit. The lab uses hand-designed vectors to demonstrate ranking, not a benchmark of semantic search quality.

Go deeper · theory, derivation and worked examples

From vector to application

Retrieval ranks documents or passages; evaluate relevance with held-out queries using measures such as recall@k. Classification feeds features into a supervised head; evaluate the actual labels on held-out data. Recommendations may learn user/item embeddings from interactions rather than text, with evaluation that respects time and avoids leaking future interactions.

Failures to look for

Averaging [good, not] can remain close to good. Mean embeddings of “dog bites person” and its reversal are exactly identical because sums commute. Static bank vectors merge senses. Domain shift, representational bias and missing terms can make neighbours misleading. Test the slices that matter, including negation and ambiguous queries; avoid treating proximity as a factual claim.

The retrieval demo explicitly averages only the known terms; it reports excluded terms and gives no score when no known tokens remain. A production system might use subwords, a learned unknown vector, or a contextual encoder instead.

The sequence-model bridge

Keep token vectors as an ordered L×d matrix. A recurrent model can update hₜ = F(hₜ₋₁,eₜ); a transformer combines token representations with position information and suitable masks. Their detailed mechanisms belong to the following lessons. Neither can recover order after a preprocessing step has already thrown it away.

A support search system should match “kitten food” to relevant cat nutrition articles. A second system must distinguish “customer refunded merchant” from “merchant refunded customer”. Design the input representation for each.

Compare your design with the discussion guide

A lexical TF–IDF baseline is cheap and interpretable but will not bridge kitten/cat without shared features. Compare an embedding-based retriever on held-out relevance queries, including rare terms, negation and domain vocabulary. State how unknowns, padding and empty queries are handled.
